## 1. The Golden Age of Piracy (c. 1650–1730)

Three phases (Rediker's "generations"):

1. **Buccaneering era (1650–1680)** — Anglo-French hunters-turned-raiders based in
   Tortuga, Hispaniola, and Jamaica preying on the Spanish Main. "Buccaneer" comes
   from *boucan*, the wooden frame hunters used to smoke meat. Culminated in Henry
   Morgan's sack of Panama City (1671). Buccaneers operated under "no prey, no pay"
   (a.k.a. "no purchase, no pay") shares.
2. **The Pirate Round (1690s)** — long voyages from the Americas to Madagascar and the
   Red Sea/Indian Ocean to rob Mughal and East India Company shipping. Thomas Tew,
   Henry Every, William Kidd. Port Royal (Jamaica) — the "wickedest city on earth,"
   the pirates' chief market — sank into the sea in the 1692 earthquake, widely read
   as divine judgment.
3. **Post-war explosion (1715–1726)** — the Treaty of Utrecht (1713) idled thousands
   of privateers and sailors; many went "on the account." This is the classic era:
   Blackbeard, Bellamy, Roberts, Bonny, Rackham, Vane. Sparked by the 1715 Spanish
   treasure-fleet wreck off Florida, whose salvage camps the future pirate captains
   raided. Effectively ended when Woodes Rogers retook Nassau (1718) and Bartholomew
   Roberts died (1722). *A General History of the Pyrates* (1724, "Captain Charles
   Johnson") is the primary source for almost everything we "know."

**Pirate vs. privateer vs. buccaneer vs. corsair**: privateers carried a *letter of
marque* — a government license to rob enemy shipping (legalized piracy; Drake, Morgan,
Kidd started this way). Corsairs = Barbary (North African) or Mediterranean raiders.
Buccaneers = the Caribbean hunters-turned-pirates. "Pirate" = no license, hostis
humani generis — "enemy of all mankind."

## 2. The great captains (guardian/boss material)

| Pirate | Epithet / hook |
|---|---|
| **Blackbeard** (Edward Teach/Thatch) | Lit slow-match fuses in his great black beard, wreathed in smoke "like a devil"; blockaded Charleston 1718; flagship *Queen Anne's Revenge* (captured French slaver *La Concorde*, ~40 guns). Killed at Ocracoke 1718 by Lt. Maynard — took ~5 pistol balls and 20 sword cuts before falling; his head hung from Maynard's bowsprit. Legend: headless body swam around the ship. |
| **Samuel "Black Sam" Bellamy** | "Prince of Pirates," self-styled "Robin Hood of the sea." Took 50+ ships in ~1 year. Flagship *Whydah Gally* (captured slave ship). Died when the Whydah sank in a nor'easter off Cape Cod, 1717; wreck found 1984 by Barry Clifford — the only fully authenticated Golden-Age pirate shipwreck, with its bell ("THE WHYDAH GALLY 1716") and treasure. Famous speech: captains of commerce "rob the poor under cover of law... we plunder the rich under the protection of our own courage." |
| **Bartholomew "Black Bart" Roberts** | Most successful: ~470 ships. Teetotaler who drank tea, dressed in crimson damask with a gold chain and diamond cross. His death at Cape Lopez (1722, grapeshot to the throat) is taken as the end of the Golden Age; his crew slipped his dressed body overboard per his wishes. His articles are the fullest surviving pirate code. |
| **Henry Every** | "King of Pirates." Captured the Mughal treasure ship *Ganj-i-Sawai* (1695) — the richest single pirate haul ever (~£600k, caused an international crisis with the Grand Mughal). Then **vanished without a trace** — perfect lore for a legendary figure. |
| **William Kidd** | Respectable NY privateer turned (arguably framed) pirate; buried treasure on Gardiners Island as a bargaining chip — recovered and used as evidence against him. Hanged at Wapping 1701 (rope broke at the first drop), body gibbeted in an iron cage over the Thames. The historical root of ALL buried-treasure/treasure-map fiction. |
| **Anne Bonny & Mary Read** | Fought disguised as men aboard Calico Jack's sloop; fiercer than most of the crew. At their trial both "pleaded the belly" (pregnancy) and escaped hanging; Read died in prison, Bonny vanishes from record. Bonny to Rackham before his hanging: "Had you fought like a man, you need not have been hang'd like a dog." |
| **Calico Jack Rackham** | Named for his calico clothing; his flag — skull over crossed cutlasses — is the modern icon. Caught because his crew was too drunk to fight. |
| **Charles Vane** | Defied the King's pardon at Nassau; fired a burning ship at Rogers' blockade. Hanged at Gallows Point, Port Royal, 1721. |
| **Stede Bonnet** | The "Gentleman Pirate" — a bored plantation owner who *bought* a ship (unheard of; pirates stole theirs) and paid wages instead of shares. Sailed with, then against, Blackbeard. Hanged at Charleston 1718. |
| **Edward Low** | The era's cruelty benchmark (torture, mutilation) — a reminder the real thing wasn't romantic. |
| **François l'Olonnais** | Buccaneer of legendary sadism; reportedly ate a victim's heart. Eaten in turn by natives. |
| **Henry Morgan** | Buccaneer admiral, sacked Panama; knighted and made Lt. Governor of Jamaica — the pirate who won. |
| **Woodes Rogers** | Ex-privateer who ended the Nassau republic: "Piracy expelled, commerce restored." |

Famous ship names: *Queen Anne's Revenge, Whydah Gally, Royal Fortune* (Roberts),
*Fancy* (Every), *Adventure Galley* (Kidd), *Revenge* (several), *Kingston, Ranger,
Flying Dragon, Royal James, Gabriel* (Low), *Satisfaction* (L'Olonnais), *Rising Sun*.

## 3. The pirate code — real, and democratic

Every crew drew up its own **articles of agreement**; each man signed (or marked) and
swore an oath — on a Bible, or lacking one, on an axe (John Phillips' men), crossed
pistols, or astride a cannon. Articles were posted on the captain's cabin door and
burned/thrown overboard when capture loomed (why so few survive). Captured artisans —
carpenters, navigators, surgeons — were forced to sign; willing recruits sometimes
*asked to be seen forced* so they could plead duress at trial.

Governance (Rediker/Leeson): crews **elected** the captain and the quartermaster;
the captain had absolute power only in battle or chase — at all other times the
**quartermaster** (also elected) was the counterweight: he divided plunder, judged
disputes, and administered punishment. Captains could be deposed by majority vote.
Share system (Roberts): captain & quartermaster 2 shares, master/boatswain/gunner
1.5, officers 1.25, crew 1 — among the most egalitarian pay schemes of the century,
plus **workman's comp**: 800 pieces of eight for a lost limb, 400 for a joint, and the
crippled man could stay aboard as long as he liked.

Bartholomew Roberts' articles (abridged, from Johnson 1724):

1. Every man a vote in affairs of moment; equal title to fresh provisions and liquor.
2. Fair turns at prize-stripping; defrauding the company of a dollar = **marooning**
   (theft between crewmates = slit ears and nose, set ashore).
3. No cards or dice for money.
4. Lights and candles out at eight; drinkers after hours take it to the open deck.
5. Keep piece, pistols, and cutlass clean and fit for service.
6. No boys or women aboard (smuggled women = death).
7. Desertion in battle = death or marooning.
8. No fighting aboard — quarrels settled ashore, back to back at pistol, then
   cutlasses to first blood, quartermaster presiding.
9. No talk of giving up the life until each man has £1,000.
10. The share system above.
11. Musicians rest on the Sabbath; the other six days, no rest without special favor.

Low/Lowther extras: he who first sights a sail gets the best pistol aboard her; good
quarter given when craved; gold found must reach the quartermaster within 24 hours.
Morgan's buccaneers: everything from the common stock, no prey no pay, and detailed
injury tariffs.

**Punishments**: marooning (left on a sand spit with a bottle of powder, a bottle of
water, a pistol), "Moses' Law" (39 lashes), slitting ears/nose, sweat-box, and —
mostly a *navy* cruelty — keelhauling. **Walking the plank is essentially fiction**
(documented at most once or twice; a 19th-century literary staple).

## 4. Flags: the Jolly Roger and its kin

- Black flag with death imagery = *parley signal*: strike your colors and live.
- **Red flag** ("jolie rouge" — a folk etymology for "Jolly Roger") = **no quarter
  given**. Pirates often ran up black first, red if refused.
- "Jolly Roger" was a generic name, not one design (per Johnson, 1724; also "Old
  Roger" — a slang name for the devil).
- Per-captain designs: **Blackbeard** — horned skeleton spearing a heart, toasting
  the devil with an hourglass. **Calico Jack** — skull over crossed cutlasses.
  **Bellamy / England / Taylor** — the now-standard skull & crossbones. **Roberts**
  (two flags) — himself astride two skulls labeled ABH ("A Barbadian's Head") and AMH
  ("A Martinican's Head"). **Every** — skull in profile with bandana & earring.
  **Hawkins' captors** (1724) — skeleton stabbing a heart with a spear. **Worley** —
  skeleton with hourglass and dart: "your time is running out."
- Common motifs: skull, whole skeleton, hourglass, bleeding heart, spear/dart, raised
  cup — all threats about time and death, meant to make crews yield without a fight.
- Tactics: false colors for the approach, true flag at pistol-shot range.

## 5. Treasure lore

- Buried pirate treasure is **nearly all myth** — pirates spent fast ("short and
  merry life"). The one well-documented burial is **Kidd's Gardiners Island cache**
  (1699), recovered by the authorities. Rumors also attach to Every's Madagascar
  loot, Blackbeard (Mulberry Island tale in Downing's 1737 history), and Rackham.
- The modern image — map, "X marks the spot," buried chest — comes from **Washington
  Irving** ("Wolfert Webber," 1824), **Poe** ("The Gold-Bug," 1843, cipher + scarab
  dropped through a skull's eye socket), and above all **Stevenson's *Treasure
  Island* (1883)**: Flint's map, the black spot, Long John Silver, the parrot
  ("pieces of eight!"), Ben Gunn marooned, "yo-ho-ho and a bottle of rum," "dead
  men's chest," "the dead men tell no tales" sentiment, singing "Fifteen men."
- Real pirate economics were **cargoes, not chests**: sugar, rum, silk, calico,
  slaves (horribly), weapons, coin. Coin = Spanish **pieces of eight** (silver peso
  de ocho reales, literally chopped into "bits" for change — origin of "two bits" =
  quarter dollar) and gold **doubloons** (2 escudos).
- Sunken treasure is the *real* lore: the **1715 Spanish Plate Fleet** (11 galleons
  drowned in a hurricane off Florida — pirates raided the salvage camps and
  jump-started the Golden Age), the **Whydah** (found 1984, the only verified pirate
  wreck), **Queen Anne's Revenge** (found 1996 in Beaufort Inlet), and the *San
  José* galleon. Oak Island: centuries of digging, nothing — treasure fever as
  cautionary tale.
- **The black spot** (a summons/sentence of the crew) and treasure maps with cryptic
  verse are Stevensonian inventions — but irresistible for the game.

## 6. Sea religion: superstitions, spirits, monsters

- **Davy Jones' locker** — the bottom of the sea; to be sent there is to drown.
  First in print 1726 (*Four Years Voyages of Capt. George Roberts*). Davy Jones
  himself: the fiend who presides over the evil spirits of the deep, seen in the
  rigging before storms and wrecks — saucer eyes, three rows of teeth, horns, blue
  smoke from his nostrils (Smollett, 1751). Etymology guesses: ghost of Jonah;
  "duppy" (West Indian ghost) + Jonah; a pub owner who pressed drunken sailors.
- **The Flying Dutchman** — ghost ship doomed to sail forever, trying to round the
  Cape of Good Hope; sighting her portends doom. Captain van der Decken swore to
  round the Cape though it took till Judgment Day.
- **The Kraken** — Norwegian legend (Bishop Pontoppidan, 1753): a sea-beast the size
  of a floating island whose back looks like skerries; when it dives, the whirlpool
  drags ships down. Later conflated with giant squid. Fish swarm above it — "if you
  had fished right, the kraken was near."
- **Mermaids/sirens** — their song wrecks sailors; manatees the likely inspiration.
- **St. Elmo's fire** — ghostly blue light on mastheads; a good omen (a saint's
  presence), though its appearance in storms made it ambiguous.
- **Albatross** — carries the soul of a dead sailor; killing one is the worst luck
  (Coleridge's *Rime of the Ancient Mariner*, 1798: the shot albatross hung round
  the mariner's neck, "water, water everywhere, nor any drop to drink").
- **Fiddler's Green** — the sailors' afterlife: music, rum, and no work.
- Bad luck: whistling aboard (whistles up wind), women aboard (the sea is jealous),
  setting sail on Friday, sharks following the ship (death aboard), renaming a ship,
  redheads and flat-footed people met on the way to the ship, killing a gull or
  albatross, saying "drowned"/"pig"/"rabbit" aboard. Good luck: a silver coin under
  the masthead, ship's cats (black best), tattoos of a compass rose or pig-and-hen
  (against drowning), children born in a caul (the caul carried = never drown).
- **Jonah** — a person whose presence brings ill luck; the remedy is throwing the
  Jonah overboard.
- Marooned men's ghosts guard buried gold — a widespread trope that a killing at the
  burial left a ghost to watch the hoard (used by Irving & Stevenson).

## 7. Drink, food, and daily life

- **Rum** was born on 17th-c. Caribbean sugar plantations from molasses waste;
  nicknames: **kill-devil**, **rumbullion**.
- **Grog** — rum cut with water (later + lime/sugar), named for Admiral Edward
  "Old Grog" Vernon (his grogram cloak), who ordered the Navy's rum diluted in 1740.
  The lime against scurvy is where "limey" comes from.
- Pirate-era mixed drinks: **bumboo/bumbo** (rum, water, sugar, nutmeg),
  **rumfustian** (raw eggs, sugar, sherry, gin, beer...), **punch**, **salmagundi**
  (a chopped hash of meat, fish, eggs, onions — pirate salad), burgoo (oatmeal gruel).
- Food aboard: hardtack ("ship's biscuit" — weevily), salt horse (salt beef), dried
  peas, cheese; water fouled in weeks, so alcohol was hydration. Turtles and
  "sea cows" at provisioning stops; buccaneers' original trade was smoking wild pig
  and cattle on Hispaniola.
- Discipline off-battle was loose; in battle, total. Drunkenness was the norm ashore:
  prizes were spent in days at Port Royal or Nassau. Musicians were prized (article
  XI above!); shanties coordinated work (capstan, halyard). Surgeons and navigators
  were the most-forced recruits.
- Weapons: cutlass (short, curved, good in close quarters), flintlock pistols
  (carried in pairs/boarding braces — Blackbeard reportedly carried six), muskets,
  boarding axes, grenadoes; ship guns: cannon prized as loot; **chain shot** to shred
  rigging, **langrage** (scrap-iron anti-rigging shot), **grapeshot** vs. crew,
  heated shot to fire ships.
- Pirate preference in ships: **sloops and schooners** — fast, shallow-draft
  (escape into shoals where warships can't follow), easy to careen (beach and scrape
  the hull); a captured frigate-like merchantman (Whydah, Queen Anne's Revenge) was
  the flagship prize. They stripped upperworks, cut gunports, reinforced hulls.

## 8. Pirate havens (port-name & lore material)

- **Nassau, New Providence** — the "Republic of Pirates" (1715–1718): Hornigold,
  Blackbeard, Vane, Rackham ruled the beach; ~1,000 pirates vs. 100 civilians.
  Ended by Woodes Rogers' pardon campaign ("piracy expelled, commerce restored").
- **Port Royal, Jamaica** — "the Sodom of the New World," richest and wickedest
  city; 1692 earthquake dumped two-thirds of it into the harbor (still down there).
- **Tortuga** — the buccaneers' rock off Hispaniola, first stronghold.
- **Madagascar** — the Pirate Round's base: Ranter Bay, St. Mary's Island (Île
  Sainte-Marie — its pirate cemetery is real), and the legend of **Libertalia**:
  Johnson's (probably invented) free colony founded by the fictional Captain Misson
  with motto "For God and Liberty" — no masters, common treasury, no slavery. David
  Graeber's *Pirate Enlightenment* (2023) argues something like it influenced real
  Malagasy society. Ideal fiction-license for a legendary isle.
- Others: **Petit-Goâve** (French), **Providence Island**, **Campeche**, **Ocracoke**
  (Blackbeard's anchorage), **Charleston** (blockaded by Blackbeard; Bonnet hanged
  there), **New Providence**, **Maracaibo** (sacked thrice), **Gardiners Island**
  (Kidd's cache).

## 9. Language — what pirates actually said (and didn't)

Authentic (period-attested): **ahoy** (hail), **avast** ("hold fast," from Dutch
*houd vast*), **aye-aye** (understood and will obey), **booty/plunder/purchase**,
**pieces of eight, doubloons, royals**, **maroon**, **parley** (truce to negotiate),
**no quarter**, **prize** (captured ship), **careen**, **letters of marque**, **on
the account** (gone pirating), **landlubber**, **scuttle**, **weigh anchor**,
**splice the mainbrace** (serve out drink), **three sheets to the wind** (drunk),
**son of a biscuit eater**, **smartly** (quickly), **handsomely** (steadily),
**strike your colors** (surrender), **give no quarter**, **run a rig** (play a
trick), **shallop/snow/brigantine** (rig types), **the sweet trade** (piracy
itself), **go on the account**, **matey** (later, plausible), **scurvy dog**,
**bilge** (nonsense), **keelhaul**, **deadlight** (eye — 19th c.), **hornpipe**,
**cat o' nine tails**, **six pounders**, **dead men tell no tales** (sentiment is
old; exact phrase 19th c.).

Pop-culture inventions: **"Arrr!"** (Robert Newton's West-Country accent in Disney's
1950 *Treasure Island*), **"shiver me timbers"** (19th-c. novels, then RLS),
**"yo-ho-ho"** (Stevenson's song), **walking the plank** (fiction), **"matey"** in
period, peg-leg + hook + eye-patch + parrot composite (Long John Silver).
Use both lists freely — players expect the Hollywood patina; period terms give it
ballast.

## 10. Ready-to-use hooks for SEAFARER

- **Guardian identities**: each of the Nine Isles could be haunted/defended by a
  legend: Blackbeard's ghost (beard of smoke), Bellamy's storm-wreck, Roberts in
  crimson damask, Every-who-vanished, Kidd's caged ghost, a Flying-Dutchman ghost
  ship, the Kraken, Davy Jones' own isle.
- **Rumor templates**: "THEY SANK THE WHYDAH OFF A LEE SHORE", "EVERY'S GOLD NEVER
  REACHED PORT", "THE RED FLAG FLIES EAST OF HERE", "AN HOURGLASS MARKS THE SPOT",
  "THE NINTH ISLE DRINKS SHIPS AT DUSK", "A CAUL CHILD CANNOT DROWN", "WHISTLE UP
  A WIND AND PAY FOR IT", "THE QUARTERMASTER KEEPS THE TRUE CHART".
- **Port-name fodder** (extends the current 16×16 tables): prefixes — GALLOWS,
  RANTER, PLATE, SCURVY, CAREEN, MAROON, FLINT, CAUL, FANCY, WHIDAH; suffixes —
  ROAD(S), ANCHORAGE, SPIT, CAUL, REEF, WATCH, ROUND, BIGHT, LEDGE, DOWN(S).
- **Flavor mechanics**: marooning as a failure state; articles/shares as a crew
  system; black flag vs. red flag (mercy vs. no quarter) as an encounter choice;
  "he who sights the sail first gets the best pistol" as a loot bonus; hourglass /
  dart / heart flag motifs as chart doodles (plan §3.4 already wants "sea monsters
  doodled at rumor cells" — Pontoppidan's kraken is the canonical doodle).
- **Dig scene dressing**: Stevenson rules — the map fragment, the landmark line
  ("fronting the landing-place are five trees" — real Downing quote about
  Blackbeard's Mulberry Island cache!), the parrot echo, the black spot as a
  guardian's curse, "dead men tell no tales" as the wreck screen's alternate line.
